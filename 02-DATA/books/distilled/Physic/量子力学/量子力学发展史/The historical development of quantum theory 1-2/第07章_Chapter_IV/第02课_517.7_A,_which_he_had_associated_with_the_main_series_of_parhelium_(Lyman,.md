Niels Bohr, to whose model Franck referred in the above remarks, had
occupied himself with an attempt to determine the structure of the helium atom
and to explain the presence of two separate spectra—the parhelium and the
'ortho-helium' spectra—since fall 1916.668 He had studied, in collaboration with
Hendrik Kramers, the problem of the mutual perturbation of the two electrons.
The results they obtained agreed, as Bohr indicated in his letter of 27 July 1919
to Sommerfeld, to some extent with those of Lande, but rested on totally
different foundations. 'In contrast to Lande we have assumed,' he wrote, 'that
though the orbit of the inner electron is eccentric, still the average value of the
energy of the inner electron (kinetic energy plus potential energy relative to the
nucleus) remains unchanged . . . That we have nevertheless obtained the same
agreement with the data as Lande, comes about due to the fact that we have
determined the stationary states in a somewhat different manner' (Bohr to
Sommerfeld, 27 July 1919). Thus Bohr and Sommerfeld did not put the average
value of the angular momentum of the outer electron equal to the integral
multiples of h/2*n (like Lande); they just assumed that the total angular
momentum possessed values of integral multiples of h/lm, while the inner electron—
because of its eccentricity—had an angular momentum slightly smaller than
h/2m. This assumption about the stationary states satisfied, as Bohr claimed, the
selection rules and the analogy argument (i.e., the correspondence principle)
relating classical and quantum theory. 'Thus Kramers and I obtain, in contrast to
Lande, a relation between the energy differences in the stationary states and the
frequencies of the harmonic oscillations, into which the motion of the external
electron can be decomposed, and this relation is analogous to the one between
the energy and the frequency of the stationary states of a simple central system,'
he concluded, and added: 'Unfortunately Kramers is sick now in Rotterdam,
and it will therefore probably be some time before we shall have the opportunity
of publishing our detailed calculations' (Bohr to Sommerfeld, 27 July 1919).
The opportunity for publishing the results of Bohr and Kramers did not come
very soon. Early in 1920 Bohr received a copy of the proof-sheets of Lande's
helium paper in the Physikalische Zeitschrift. In a letter to Lande, Bohr discussed
the relation of Lande's results to his own and Kramers', especially the deviations
between them, and stated: 'On reading through your paper I have arrived at the
opinion that this deviation arises from the difference in the approximation
methods, and that your treatment is not particularly suited for the purpose under
consideration' (Bohr to Lande, 24 February 1920). He then argued that Lande
had not considered all the terms necessary for his approximation; however, had
he done so, he would have arrived at exactly the same results as he—Bohr—and
Kramers had already obtained. Later on, Bohr invited Lande—who was
personally very interested in visiting Copenhagen—to present his ideas on the spatial

In spite of the fact that Bohr and Kramers published no paper on their helium
calculations in the years between 1916 and 1922, interested physicists like
Sommerfeld, Lande and Ehrenfest got to know the basic assumptions and results
of the Copenhagen approach. Thus, for example, Bohr explained to Ehrenfest the
main differences between his and Lande's approaches:
In my opinion the reason for the occurrence of the two separate helium spectra
cannot be sought for in a simple spatial quantization of a continuously connected
class of motions, as Lande has assumed in his paper. First, a similar situation would
show up also in the case of other atoms; and second, combinations between the two
spectra would not be excluded on the basis of such an interpretation [as Lande's]. It
is exactly this absence of such combinations—even when external fields of force,
which cause some of the missing combination lines within each of the two spectra,
are absent—, which indicates to me beyond any doubt that one has to conclude
from the two spectra the existence of two entirely different types of motion,
separated from one another by forms of motion that cannot be sharply quantized,
such as the ones investigated by Kramers and myself. (Bohr to Ehrenfest, 28 March
1921)
Bohr gave a detailed account of his and Kramers' helium models in an
unpublished manuscript of spring 1921 (which was connected with the report which
Bohr intended to give at the third Solvay Conference at Brussels in April 1921).
In this manuscript he devoted a full section to the helium problem (Bohr, 1977.
pp. 122-139). He noted that the investigations of many possible motions of the
two-electron atom had led Kramers and himself to consider only two simple,
periodic cases: 'In the first of these cases the electrons move through all times in
one and the same plane, and in the second the planes of the orbits form with a
high degree of approximation constantly a right angle with each other, the major
axis of the inner orbit remaining perpendicular to the plane of the outer orbit'
(Bohr, 1977, p. 125).
