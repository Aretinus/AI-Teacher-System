to improve on the approximations made in the calculation of the energy states of
helium. 'The progress in comparison to the earlier work,' he remarked in the
introduction, 'consists in systematically taking into account the orbital

To start with, Lande rearranged the Hamiltonian of the coplanar helium
system into the form of a sum, consisting of three terms: the first term describing
the energy of the inner electron in the field of the nucleus, the second the same
part of the energy of the outer electron (i.e., its kinetic energy plus the potential
energy due to a screened nucleus of charge \e\), and the third the remaining
periodic perturbation energy. In order to obtain the energy terms, Lande used his
new 'adiabatic' quantization method of perturbed systems, which he had
discussed in an earlier paper (Lande, 1919d). This method allowed one to work with
the known quantization rules of the unperturbed orbits, provided one could
transform the latter via adiabatic changes into perturbed orbits. Applying it to
the helium problem with two electron orbits, Lande first looked at a system in
which the outer electron was removed completely (i.e., it moved on an orbit
having an infinite radius), and then changed it infinitely slowly to the situation in
which it (i.e., the outer electron) was bound in an orbit of finite radius. Then he
calculated the corresponding changes in the energy terms, and obtained the
following result: the change in the energy of the inner electron could be
neglected as being very small; the energy of the outer electron could be expressed
as the sum of the energy it had in the Coulomb field of a hydrogen nucleus plus
terms of order (2r0/R0)5/3 and higher powers, r0 being the lowest radius of the
ionized helium and R0 the radius of the outer electron in the field of the
hydrogen nucleus. Evidently, the calculated energy values were reliable, except
the energy value for the state in which the outer electron assumed a one-quantum
(i.e., n = 1) orbit.

Lande's theory of the helium atom represented an important step in the
treatment of the problem. It was followed by a lively activity of the physicists,
who explored more deeply and in detail the experimental and theoretical
properties of the two-electron atom. It began with the work of James Franck and Paul
Knipping, who made a new determination of the ionization potential of helium,
namely, 25.4 ± 0.25 V (Franck and Knipping, 1919, p. 486).666 This value
contradicted the theoretical values derived either from Bohr's helium model (with
the two electrons on the same one-quantum ring around the nucleus) or from
Lande's (and Epstein-Sommerfeld's) model for the normal helium atom with the
coplanar orbits: the former yielded an ionization potential of 28.77 V and the
latter roughly 30 V. James Franck and Fritz Reiche concluded, therefore, 'that
the measured value belongs to the less stable helium atom with the crossed electron
orbits, and that it is this atom {i.e., the parhelium) which we have as normal helium
in the non-excited state' (Franck and Reiche, 1920, p. 155). As a consequence,
normal (i.e., ortho-) helium with coplanar orbits could occur only in a state
where the outer electron moves on a two-quantum orbit, which represents the
lowest state of this system. Franck and Knipping then showed that the electron
collision experiments supported the existence of a 'metastable' helium state; it
possessed larger energy than the lowest state of parhelium, but could not pass
over into it by the emission of radiation (Franck and Knipping, 1920). Edwin C.
Kemble objected strongly to this conclusion concerning the ground state of
helium: first, he claimed that Franck and Reiche's interpretation of the helium
ground state was difficult to reconcile with Irving Langmuir's assumptions on the
structure of helium (Langmuir, 1919b), which accounted well for the chemical
properties of the noble gas (see our discussion in Section III.3); in fact, an
electron moving on an outer orbit—as assumed by Franck and Reiche—would
not be consistent with the observed stability of helium; second, he proved that
the observed lowest term did not correspond to the Sj-term of Lande; third, on
the basis of the recently observed ultraviolet spectrum of helium, he concluded
against the assumption that the ground state of helium should be a 1 S-state, i.e.,
an S-state with circular orbit (Kemble, 1921). In order to resolve these
difficulties, Kemble proposed another model, in which the coordinates of the two
electrons (denoted by subscripts 1 and 2) satisfied a symmetry condition with
