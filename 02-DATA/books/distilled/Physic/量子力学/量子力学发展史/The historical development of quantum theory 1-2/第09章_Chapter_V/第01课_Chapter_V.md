# Chapter V

The Problems of Radiation Theory
At the very origin of quantum theory had stood a special problem of radiation
theory, that of blackbody radiation. While Max Planck had based the derivation
of his blackbody radiation formula in 1900 on the assumption that radiation
could be treated as electromagnetic waves described by classical electrodynamics
(Planck, 1900f), Albert Einstein showed in 1905 that at least some radiation
exhibited properties similar to those of material particles, which he tried to
account for by what he called the light-quantum hypothesis (Einstein, 1905b).
With this hypothesis Einstein immediately obtained a reasonable interpretation
of several radiation phenomena, especially the so-called photoelectric effect. In
1913 Niels Bohr used an equation, modelled after Einstein's equation relating the
kinetic energy of the photoelectron to the frequency of the incident ultraviolet
light, Eq. (60), as one of the fundamental hypotheses of the theory of atomic
structure: namely, the frequency condition, Eq. (118). In spite of these and other
successful applications of the light-quantum concept, the large majority of the
physicists working on quantum theory did not believe in it. They subscribed to
what Planck, Nernst, Rubens and Warburg wrote in 1913 about Einstein: 'that
he may have sometimes missed the target in his speculations as, for example, in
his theory of light-quanta.'803 And during the following decade hardly anyone
took light-quanta seriously until there appeared a paper to settle this question
completely: this was the paper presented by Arthur Holly Compton in December
1922 (Compton, 1923b). The main result of this paper—the so-called Compton
effect—demanded almost inevitably an interpretation of the radiation as
consisting of light-quanta of definite energy and directed momentum, which created
great difficulties in Bohr's theory of atomic structure, for it could not be made to
agree with the correspondence principle. Therefore, Bohr and his collaborators,
Hendrik Kramers and John Slater, proposed in early 1924 a new radiation
theory: especially, they stuck to the classical description of radiation as
electromagnetic waves and explained the Compton effect as being due to the peculiar
interaction of radiation with atoms in which the laws of energy and momentum
conservation were satisfied only on the statistical average (Bohr, Kramers and
Slater, 1924). Before one could prove or disprove the Bohr-Kramers-Slater
theory experimentally, the light-quantum hypothesis received some theoretical

leaving only the light-quantum theory of Einstein and Bose as a consistent
quantum theory of radiation. In spite of this success, however, the quantum
theory of radiation raised serious conceptual problems in relation to the classical
description of radiation and with respect to the thermodynamic properties of
radiation and matter. These problems would continue to plague the physicists
beyond the period which is treated in this book. The quantum theory of radiation
would again become a subject of serious discussions in the years following the
discovery of quantum mechanics and wave mechanics. In the beginning of that
period Gilbert N. Lewis (1926) would give a new name—'photon'—to the
light-quantum, in order to emphasize its fundamental role—besides that of
electrons and protons—in the structure of atoms.
V.l The Proof of the Light-Quantum Hypothesis
When Albert Einstein introduced in 1905 what he called a 'heuristic point of
view' or the 'light-quantum' into radiation theory, he had in mind to explain
three different radiation phenomena: the high frequency, low density part of
blackbody radiation described by Wilhelm Wien's law of 1896; the so-called rule
of George Gabriel Stokes stating that the frequency of fluorescent light was
always smaller than the frequency of radiation exciting it; and the principal
aspects of the photoelectric effect, i.e., the facts that the velocity of the ejected
electrons depended only on the frequency of the incident (ultraviolet) radiation,
and their number on its intensity. Certainly, the light-quantum hypothesis
accounted for all these phenomena; however, since this hypothesis contradicted
completely the conventional concepts of radiation theory, the question had to be
asked whether it was really necessary. In the case of blackbody radiation, for
example, Planck argued that the classical undulatory description sufficed and
that the quantum effects just arose from the peculiar interaction between
radiation and the elementary resonators (or material particles) in a cavity
(Planck, 1913). Although the attempts of Planck and others to use such an idea
for obtaining a consistent derivation of the blackbody radiation law were not

the undulatory theory of radiation confirmed by the discovery of the phenomena
of interference of X-rays in crystals; with it Max von Laue, Walter Friedrich and
Paul Knipping settled a debate, which had been going on since 1895, in favour of
X-rays being electromagnetic waves of extremely short wavelengths (Friedrich,
Knipping and Laue, 1912).
The question of testing the light-quantum versus the undulatory theory of
radiation seemed to be even more difficult in the case of the other phenomena, to
which Einstein had addressed himself, because of two reasons: first, both
fluorescence and the photoelectric effect involved the interaction of radiation
and matter, an interaction which was little known; second, not enough accurate
or detailed data on fluorescence and the photoelectric effect were available in
order to decide clearly for or against the light-quantum hypothesis. However,
towards the end of the first and during the second decade of the twentieth
century, both experimental and theoretical investigations were carried out by
many physicists to decide this question. For example, Charles Glover Barkla
found (Barkla, 1908b)—confirmed later by Ernst Wagner and others—that in the
case of X-ray fluorescence the frequency of the (homogeneous) secondary
radiation was also smaller than that of the primary radiation exciting it, in
agreement with Stokes' rule and Einstein's prediction. The linear relation
between the maximal kinetic energy of the ejected photoelectron and the frequency
of the incident ultraviolet radiation, Eq. (60), was tested in detail and confirmed
completely, especially by the work of Robert Andrews Millikan (1916a, b). At
the same time Millikan remarked: 'Yet the semi-corpuscular theory by which
Einstein arrived at this equation seems at present to be wholly untenable'
(Millikan, 1916b, p. 383). He argued that Einstein's light-quantum hypothesis
had to be considered as a special case of Joseph John Thomson's theory,
according to which radiation had to be represented by localized trains of
electromagnetic energy (Thomson, 1904c). Such a fibrous structure of the
electromagnetic field, he claimed, should extend also to static fields; then, however, the
result of the oil-drop experiment—i.e., Millikan's own experiment to measure the
charge of the electron (Millikan, 1913)—namely, that the force exerted by a static
electric field on an electron was always proportional to the field strength, could
not be understood. He concluded: 'If then we must abandon the Thomson-
Einstein hypothesis of localized energy . . . , there is no alternative but to assume
that the corpuscles which are ejected are already possessed of an energy almost equal
to hv' (Millikan, 1916b, p. 385). As a necessary consequence, Millikan argued, the
energy must come from the incident radiation and be transferred to the electrons
by oscillations of the charged constituents (electrons and ions) of the atoms in
the absorbing metal's surface; the oscillation energy would then accumulate until
the amount hv was reached, and this amount would be transferred to the ejected
photoelectron. Such a theory had been proposed already, for example, by Peter
Debye and Arnold Sommerfeld (1913) and by Max Planck (1913). In spite of the
