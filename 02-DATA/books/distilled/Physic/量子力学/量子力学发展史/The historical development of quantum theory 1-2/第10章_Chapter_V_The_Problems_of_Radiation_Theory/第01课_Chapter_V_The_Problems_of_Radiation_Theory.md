As we have discussed in Chapter I, Einstein had tried hard to develop since
1905 a consistent radiation theory on the basis of the light-quantum hypothesis.
Especially, he had analyzed the energy fluctuations in blackbody radiation
(obeying Planck's law) in order to determine the properties of light-quanta
(Einstein, 1909a, b). He had thus found that the fluctuations had a twofold
origin: one part arose from the wave theory, while the other part—describing
the situation for large frequencies and small intensities (i.e., the region in
which Wien's law was valid)—could be explained as the 'crudest illustration of
the light-quantum hypothesis' ('roheste Veranschaulichung der
Lichtquantenhypothese'), namely, the assumption that radiation consisted of 'objects of very
small extension having the energy hv, which propagate in space independently of
each other, and are reflected [say, from a wall] independently of each other'
(Einstein, 1909b, p. 824). Einstein had thus arrived at the following conclusion:
'Besides the spatial non-uniformity in the distribution of the momentum of radiation
due to the undulatory theory, there exists yet another non-uniformity in the spatial
distribution of the momentum, which—for low energy density of the radiation—
dominates the former non-uniformity by far' (Einstein, 1909b, p. 824).
While Einstein had been rather careful in using the idea of free, independent
light-quanta, Johannes Stark, the other leading advocate of the light-quantum
hypothesis, had gone ahead and considered in some detail the process of
interaction between light-quanta (of energy hv and directed momentum of
magnitude hv/c) and matter. Thus Stark, in a paper submitted in November
1909—i.e., shortly after Einstein's talk at Salzburg, in which he had presented the
above-mentioned conclusion about the momentum of radiation—had treated the
production of X-rays by the impact of electrons on the anticathode by taking
into account energy and momentum conservation between the electron and a
single light-quantum (Stark, 1909d). Stark's explanation of X-ray production had
been criticized, as we have discussed in Section 1.5, by Arnold Sommerfeld.
Sommerfeld showed that the observed asymmetry in the intensity distribution of
X-rays could be accounted for perfectly by the classical wave theory
(Sommerfeld, 1909b). Then, after the discovery of the X-ray interference phenomena in
1912, the extreme light-quantum hypothesis of Einstein and Stark had been

analogous to the laws holding, according to the quantum theory, for collisions

By the last remark Bohr meant that many empirical facts, such as the
polarization rules for the radiation emitted by atoms, seemed to imply (for example, in
the derivation of Rubinowicz, 1921) the use of the light-quantum hypothesis in
its most extreme version: that is, one had to assume a special coupling of atoms
and the radiation field, in which the collisions between electrons and quanta of
radiation played a fundamental role.
Bohr evidently did not favour the light-quantum hypothesis, for it seemed to
ruin his plans to build atomic theory on the basis of the correspondence
principle. Thus, in his lecture on 27 April 1920 at the German Physical Society in
Berlin, he remarked:
I shall not here discuss the familiar difficulties to which the "hypothesis of
light-quanta" leads in connection with the phenomena of interference, for the
explanation of which the classical theory of radiation has shown itself to be so
remarkably suited. Above all, I shall not consider the problem of the nature of
radiation. I shall only attempt to show how it has been possible in a purely formal
manner to develop a spectral theory, the essential elements of which may be
considered as a simultaneous rational development of the two ways of interpreting
Planck's result [i.e., the law of blackbody radiation]. (Bohr, 1920, pp. 425-426;
Collected Works 3, 1976, p. 244)
While Bohr avoided entering into the problem of radiation theory as much as
possible in his talk, he took the opportunity of discussing it privately with Albert
Einstein on that occasion. Einstein at that time was thinking again about the
consequences of the quantum-theoretical absorption and emission process. Thus,
a few months before Bohr's visit to Berlin, he had written to Born:
That business about causality causes me a lot of trouble, too. Can the quantum
absorption and emission of light ever be understood in the sense of the complete
causality requirement or would a statistical residue remain? I must admit that here I
lack the courage of my convictions. But I would be very unhappy to renounce
complete causality. (Einstein to Born, 27 January 1920)
Evidently Einstein, like Bohr, struggled with the problems of quantum-theoretical
radiation. He even believed that the problem was more serious and difficult and

could not be overcome completely by methods such as the ones offered by the
analogy or the correspondence principle. Still, it was good for Bohr to talk about
his ideas and worries with Einstein. Several weeks later he thanked Einstein for
the conversations. He wrote: 'You don't know how stimulating it was for me to
have the opportunity, which I had hoped for since long, to hear personally your
opinion about the problems I have been dealing with. I shall never forget our
conversations on the way from Dahlem to your house, and I hope very much
that when you visit us here there will be an opportunity to continue these
conversations' (Bohr to Einstein, 24 June 1920). However, Einstein's visit to
Copenhagen, which was planned and to which Bohr looked forward for
continuing his discussions on the problems of quantum theory, did not take place. Both
of them were invited to participate in the third Solvay Conference in Brussels in
April 1921, but both were prevented from attending it and meeting again.
Therefore, Bohr and Einstein were not able to exchange their views further for

atomic scale, because even the kinetic theory of gases had been built on the strict
validity of energy and momentum conservation. On the other hand, nobody had
been able to test the conservation laws directly on the atomic level. Thus the

independence between transition processes in distant atoms than I perceived'

introductory sentence as: 'Any atom may, in fact, be supposed to communicate
with other atoms all the time it is in a stationary state, by means of a virtual field

quantum transitions and the function of which is to provide for statistical
conservation of energy and momentum by determining the probabilities for

quantum transitions' (Slater, 1924a, p. 307). Thus the concept of statistical
conservation laws sneaked into Slater's note.835

details of the mechanism underlying the process of transition between two
stationary states. Bohr concluded: 'At the present state of science it seems

Such probability considerations had been introduced by Einstein in 1916, and
they had played a crucial role in the recent discussion of the temperature
equilibrium between free electrons or atoms and radiation, carried out by Pauli
and by Einstein and Ehrenfest. In spite of the inclusion of probability
considerations, the quantum-theoretical description of radiation phenomena had to
represent a 'natural generalization' of the classical, electrodynamic description, as
was 'evident from the condition that in the limit, where we consider processes

which depend on the statistical behaviour of the large number of atoms, and
which involve stationary states where the difference between neighbouring
stationary states is comparatively little, the classical theory always leads to
conclusions in agreement with experiment' (Bohr, Kramers and Slater, 1924, p. 789). In
Bohr's opinion the correspondence principle accounted completely for the
relation established thus far between the classical and the quantum theory of
radiation. However, the difficulties to account, say, for the 'time interval in which
the emission of radiation connected with the transition takes place . . . has
strengthened the doubt. . . whether the detailed interpretation of the interaction
between matter and radiation can be given at all in terms of a causal description
in space and time of the kind hitherto used for the interpretation of natural
phenomena' (Bohr, Kramers and Slater, 1924, p. 790).
In section two of the paper, Slater's ideas on the radiation of atoms, as
modified by the considerations of Kramers, were discussed. Thus the authors,
i.e., Bohr and Kramers, began by saying: 'We will assume that a given atom in a
certain stationary state will communicate continually with other atoms through a
time-spatial mechanism which is virtually equivalent with the field of radiation
which on the classical theory would originate from the virtual harmonic
oscillators corresponding with the various possible transitions to other stationary states'
(Bohr, Kramers and Slater, 1924, p. 790). The authors further assumed that the
occurrence of transition processes (absorption and emission) in the given atom
and in the atoms 'with which it is in mutual communication' were connected 'by
probability laws which are analogous to those which in Einstein's theory hold for
the induced transitions between stationary states when illuminated by radiation'
(loc. cit., p. 791). They interpreted the 'spontaneous' transitions of Einstein as
'induced by the virtual field of radiation which is connected with the virtual
harmonic oscillators conjugated with the motion of the atom itself,' and the
'induced' transitions as arising 'in consequence of the virtual radiation in the
surrounding space due to other atoms' (loc. cit., p. 791). Such an interpretation,
the authors concluded, implied a change in the usual space-time picture of the
radiation processes, especially because the occurrence of a certain transition in a
given atom not only depended on its initial state (as in the usual picture) but also
on the states of the atoms with which the given atom 'communicated' through
the virtual radiation field. While this conclusion was in accord with Slater's
original ideas (concerning the linewidths of radiation emitted by atoms), the
following assumption went beyond them. The authors declared: 'As regards the
occurrence of transitions [in atoms], which is the essential feature of the quantum
theory, we abandon . . . any attempt at a causal connexion between the
transitions in distant atoms, and especially a direct application of the principles of
conservation of energy and momentum, so characteristic for the classical
theories' (Bohr, Kramers and Slater, 1924, p. 791). That is, strict conservation
principles should be applied only to collisions between the atoms, where the
forces arising from the interaction between radiation and matter (atoms or
electrons) could be neglected against the electrostatic forces of attraction and
repulsion between the atoms involved in the collision.

V.2 The Bohr-Kramers-Slater Theory of Radiation
Besides the assumption of the statistical conservation of energy and
momentum for atomic processes involving absorption and emission of radiation, a
second, to some extent related, assumption was proposed by the authors, an
assumption which could again be motivated by the correspondence principle.
According to the classical theory of radiation, distant atoms should not exert
upon each other an instantaneous influence. Therefore, Bohr and Kramers
demanded that 'an induced transition in an atom is not directly caused by a
transition in a distant atom for which the energy difference between the initial
and final stationary state is the same' (Bohr, Kramers and Slater, 1924, p. 792).
That is, the processes of emission and absorption in distant atoms must be
regarded as being statistically independent.
The authors were fully aware of the fact that so far no empirical tests existed
of the two assumptions, namely, the statistical energy and momentum
conservation and the statistical independence of radiation processes in distant atoms.
However, they claimed that their new theory of radiation did not contradict any
available information and, in fact, allowed one, without 'any departure from the
electrodynamic theory of light as regards the propagation of radiation in free
space,' 'to establish a consistent and fairly complete description of the general
optical phenomena accompanying the propagation of light through a material
medium, which accounts at the same time for the close connexion of these
phenomena with the spectra of the atoms of the medium' (Bohr, Kramers and
Slater, 1924, pp. 793-794). They presented the outline of such a consistent
description in the last two sections of their paper.
In section three, Bohr, Kramers and Slater discussed what they called the
'capacity of interference of spectral lines,' i.e., the ability of a given spectral line,
originating from an atom which undergoes a transition from one stationary state
to another, to interfere with itself. They claimed that the sharpness (linewidth) of
the spectral lines and the 'capacity of interference' had to be related in atomic
theory, and further that the sharpness of a spectral line was determined by the
accuracy with which the energies of the initial and the final states of the atoms
could be defined. The fact that both states contributed then established the
observed 'reciprocity' existing in 'the constitution of a line when appearing in an
emission and in an absorption spectrum' (Bohr, Kramers and Slater, 1924, p.
795), in agreement with Kirchhoff's law of the emission and absorption of
radiation in thermal equilibrium.838

During the first fifteen years of its existence the light-quantum hypothesis was
applied only to a few selected problems: the derivation of Planck's law of
blackbody radiation (Einstein, 1906c, 1916e), the explanation of the photoelectric
effect (Einstein, 1905b) and the description of the production of X-rays (Stark,
1909b). A really universal theory combining the usual radiation phenomena and
these quantum phenomena on the basis of the light-quantum concept appeared
to be impossible, because the existence of interference effects seemed to prevent
it. Indeed, all the processes involving the propagation of light, its reflection and
diffraction could be described perfectly by the assumption that radiation
consisted of transverse electromagnetic waves, and one could not imagine any
method of dealing with them as well by a nonundulatory hypothesis. By late
1922, however, the situation had changed drastically. Compton recognized that
the shift in the wavelength of X-rays, if scattered by atoms, could be derived only
by assuming the existence of radiation in the form of light-quanta, which collide
with the electrons in atoms in elementary processes (Compton, 1923a, b). While
he did not believe in the reality of the Compton effect, William Duane of
Harvard University suggested the existence of another light-quantum effect in
1923, with the help of which he explained what he called 'abnormal reflection' of

The basic method, developed by Bose in his derivation of Planck's law
outlined above, was a consistent use of the division of the quantum-theoretical
phase space of the light-quanta in cells of volume h3. Such a method had been
suggested earlier for material particles or molecules by Otto Sackur (1911) and
especially by Max Planck (1915b, c). However, none of Bose's predecessors had
given the simple solution obtained by the physicist from Dacca, which implied
the two simple steps: first, to evaluate the number of cells in phase space as the
number representing the possible sites of the quantum-theoretical objects under
investigation; second, to determine the probability of a given distribution by
counting the number of realizations of this distribution among the possible sites.

considered this radiation as emerging from the interaction between resonators

radiation. Bose, a quarter of a century later, adopted Einstein's point of view by
treating ensembles of free light-quanta. He recognized that light-quanta could be
considered as relativistic massless particles (see Eq. (296)). In agreement with
Planck's procedure, however, he assumed that light-quanta were

('Remarks on the Quantum Mechanics of Free Electrons') and dated 18 July
1925, appeared in Naturwissenschaften'* issue of 14 August (Elsasser, 1925).954 In
it, Elsasser proposed the assumption that electrons of low velocities, like the
atoms in Einstein's gas theory, exhibited wave effects—effects which would be
easily accessible to experiment due to the small mass of the electrons.955 This
assumption, he remarked further, made connection with certain existing
experiments on the behaviour of slow electrons. Especially, he pointed to the following
observations:
If one attempts to interpret the peculiar dependence of the mean free path of
electrons [on their velocity]—which Ramsauer and, after him, many other authors
have found—on the above-mentioned [phase-wave] hypothesis, then one notices
that the curves representing this dependence show a very remarkable agreement
with curves which one obtains on the classical theory for the diffraction of light by
colloidal spheres . . . There seem to be indications also for interference according
to an experiment of Davisson and Kunsman [1923], in which the authors
investigated the angular distribution of electrons reflected from a platinum plate.
(Elsasser, 1925, p. 711)
Elsasser mentioned that both effects could be described qualitatively or
approximately by applying de Brogue's Eq. (334)—especially, the shift of Davisson and
Kunsman's intensity maxima with increasing velocity of the electrons impinging
on the platinum surface. Finally, he remarked that for a quantitative
confirmation one had to await 'further experiments, which are in preparation at the
present time' (Elsasser, 1925, p. 711).
Elsasser persuaded Franck to let him investigate the problem experimentally.
'Franck was very generous,' he recalled later. T tried for two or three months to
design an experiment to find those things. Franck said I could have access to his
workshop and so forth, but that he was not able to take any of his more
experienced men out of their work to help me, which is what I had asked him to
do. So I gave up after I think three months because this is a very difficult job for
a 21-year-old student who has no experience' (Elsasser, AHQP Interview, 29
May 1962, p. 2). The experiment, which Elsasser had hoped to carry out, was

One may ask why the confirmation of what Elsasser had suggested in July
1925 had to wait for such a long time. Why, for example, did James Franck not
support Elsasser—when he knew that he was not an experienced experimentalist
—more effectively? Perhaps the reason lay in the general attitude taken by the
physicists in Göttingen. As Heisenberg recalled:
At least Franck . . . started to propagate the idea of matter waves. Born, in some
way, was very skeptical and said: "Well, that's too odd. That's absurd. That now
the electron which we can see in a cloud chamber is to be a wave—what do you
mean by that?" Then, of course, the discussion came to the Compton effect where
we see that kind of absurdity—we have to have light-quanta as well as waves.
(Heisenberg, Conversations, p. 244; also AHQP Interview)
That is, in summer 1925 the connection between matter and waves appeared
to be still a vague talk; people assumed that there might be some truth in it, but
they did not dare to have more definite opinions. Similarly, the situation of
radiation theory was also not clear. Only several months later would decisive
progress take place: Erwin Schrödinger, after concerning himself with Einstein's
gas theory (Schrödinger, 1926b), would come out with a new kind of quantum
theory based on the wave concept in a series of papers (Schrödinger, 1926c, d,e,
f). His work would then stimulate the experimentalists to test Louis de Broglie's
phase-wave theory of matter. But these events belong to a later chapter in the
development of quantum mechanics.
