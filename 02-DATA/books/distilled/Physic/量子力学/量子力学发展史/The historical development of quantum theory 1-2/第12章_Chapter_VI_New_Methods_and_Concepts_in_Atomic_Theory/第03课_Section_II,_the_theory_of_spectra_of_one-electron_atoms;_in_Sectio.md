organization. All authors devoted the earlier parts of their reviews—e.g., Section
I of Pauli's article, Chapters One and Two of Born's book, and Chapters I to IV
of Van Vleck's report—to setting up the foundations of the quantum theory of
atomic structure; then they treated one-electron atoms and several-electron
atoms. However, within this general organization, each author presented the
material according to his own taste and preferences. Thus, for instance, Born—
after briefly mentioning the physical foundations of quantum theory
(Introduction)—gave a detailed description of the classical Hamilton-Jacobi theory
and its application to multiply periodic systems (Chapters One and Two). Then
he treated one-electron atoms with or without external fields (Chapter Three),
and finally he developed systematically the quantum-theoretical perturbation
schemes for multiply periodic systems (Chapter Four). In general, Born restricted
his efforts to discussing 'the facts of atomic physics from the special viewpoint of
the application of mechanical principles' (Born, 1925, p. V). Since the application
of these mechanical principles had been recognized in the previous couple of
years (i.e., between 1922 and 1924) as leading to several fundamental failures—
such as the failure in the calculation of the energy states of the helium atom, or
in the description of the anomalous Zeeman effects, or in the crossed-field
problem—Born cautiously called his Vorlesungen über Atommechanik the 'first
volume' of a series of books on the quantum theory that he intended to write. In
particular, in the preface he announced a second volume dealing with 'a higher
approximation to the "final" atomic mechanics' (Born, 1925, p. V).1078
In fall 1924 Born was very cautious about a more 'final' atomic mechanics. He
argued that 'at the moment one possesses only a few unclear hints about the type
of deviations, which must be imposed upon the classical laws in order to explain
atomic properties' (Born, 1925, p. V), referring to Heisenberg's recent theory of
the anomalous Zeeman effects (Heisenberg, 1924e), the Bohr-Kramers-Slater
theory of radiation (Bohr, Kramers and Slater, 1924) and Kramers' dispersion
theory (Kramers, 1924a, b). While Born did not discuss these matters in the
Vorlesungen, the other authors devoted several sections to them. Thus Van Vleck
not only wrote a chapter on 'The Correspondence Principle for Intensities'
(Chapter IX), but also one on 'Polarization, Dispersion, etc., in the Quantum
Theory' (Chapter X), 'Half Quanta and the Anomalous Zeeman Effect' (Chapter
XII) and 'Light-Quanta' (Chapter XIII), all of them dealing with developments
going beyond the limits of the old quantum theory of atomic structure. As a rule,
