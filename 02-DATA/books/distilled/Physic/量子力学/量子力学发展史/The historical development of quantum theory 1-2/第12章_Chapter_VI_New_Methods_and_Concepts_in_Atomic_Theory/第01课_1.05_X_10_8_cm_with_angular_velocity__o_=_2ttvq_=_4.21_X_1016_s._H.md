Darwin's theory stimulated the interest of Niels Bohr. Bohr had known for
many years that the theory of dispersion of radiation by atoms and molecules, as
developed by Debye and Sommerfeld in 1915, had failed to account for essential
facts.963 But he had not been able to attack the dispersion problem himself, for
his theory of atomic structure seemed to offer no means of approaching it. Thus
he had avoided entering into the discussion of dispersion for nearly a decade.
Even in his paper on the fundamental postulates of quantum theory, submitted
in November 1922 to Zeitschrift für Physik, Bohr still stressed the difficulties
hindering the progress towards a consistent quantum theory of the dispersion of
light. There he remarked: 'It seems necessary, in order to account for the
phenomena of reflection and dispersion, to assume that an atom reacts on the
field of radiation just as a system of electrified particles in the classical theory—
in other words, that the atom forms the starting point for a secondary wave train
which stands in a coherent phase relation with the original field of radiation.'
However, this necessity conflicted with the observation that 'it is an immediate
consequence of the requirements typical to the quantum theory for the stability
of the stationary states, that the reaction of the atom to the influence of a field of
radiation in general cannot even approximately be calculated according to the
classical theory' (Bohr, 1923a; English translation, Bohr, 1976, p. 495). The
conflict between the classical and the quantum-theoretical description showed
itself in the fact that the characteristic frequencies of the radiation emitted or
absorbed by atoms were not connected in any simple way with the motion of the
electrons in the normal state of the atom. Hence Bohr claimed that the reaction
of the atom on being subjected to incident radiation arises from 'an unknown
mechanism which is answerable for the emission of radiation on the transition
between stationary states' (Bohr, loc. cit., p. 496). That mechanism, he argued,
had also to take into account that 'the real absorption [of radiation by atoms]
does not merely consist in a uniform decrease in the energy of the wave train [of
the incident light], but in an exchange of energy, governed by discontinuous
laws, between individual atoms and the field of radiation' (Bohr, loc. cit., p. 496).
According to Bohr, in a quantum-theoretical description of the dispersion of
light by atoms there would not exist any phase coherence between the absorbed
and the reemitted radiation, as was exhibited in the phenomenon of resonance
radiation. Therefore he criticized, in a footnote in his paper quoted above,
Darwin's picture of the dispersion process—which established a certain phase
relation between incident and emitted radiation—as being 'scarcely reconcilable
with the assumption of a finite duration of the existence of excited atoms' and
encountering 'apparently insuperable difficulties when it tries to take account of
the phenomena of dispersion in very weak illumination' (Bohr, 1923a; English

Another property of spectral lines, which had thus far not been possible to
describe satisfactorily in atomic theory, was their intensity. In classical
electrodynamics the intensity of radiation emerging from an accelerated charge followed
immediately from its (the charge's) electromagnetic field. Thus, for example, for
an oscillating electron—and an electron moving in an elliptical orbit around a
nucleus could be represented by an oscillating electron—the intensity depended
only on the amplitude and the frequency of the oscillation. In Bohr's quantum
theory of atomic structure, classical electrodynamics had failed. Neither the
frequency of the emitted radiation was given by the motion of the electron nor
the intensity of the lines could be related to it: the intensity rather depended on
the probability for the electrons to occupy the initial state involved in the
transition, and on the tendency or probability of the initial state to pass over into
the final state. The first of these probabilities—i.e., the probability to occupy a
given state—was not a simple quantity either because it also depended on the
statistical weight of the state.
In the early years of Bohr's theory there did not really exist a reliable method
of calculating the intensities of spectral lines. Nevertheless the theoreticians tried.
The first to develop some detailed ideas about the question was Arnold
Sommerfeld: he suggested that the intensity depended on the number of possibilities to
obtain a given spectral line by means of transitions between different states; that
is, the intensity was just proportional to number of transitions between the two
orbits involved in the emission of the line under investigation. Further, he
claimed that very eccentric orbits were less probable than the circular ones
belonging to the same principal quantum number (Sommerfeld, 1916b).
Although Sommerfeld was able to describe the data on the intensities of spectral
lines and their components—e.g., the relativistic or Stark components—at least
qualitatively, Niels Bohr did not like his ideas. In his 1918 memoirs Bohr put
forward a different explanation by claiming that a close relation existed between
the radiation emitted by electrons moving in Kepler orbits on the classical theory
and the radiation emitted by atoms, provided transitions between neighbouring
states of high quantum numbers were involved (Bohr, 1918a). Hendrik Kramers
then quite successfully calculated on the basis of Bohr's analogy or
correspondence argument the intensities of hydrogen lines, especially of their Stark and
Zeeman components (Kramers, 1919). In evaluating the frequencies, p , and the
amplitudes, A , of the spectral lines, he took averages of the corresponding

photographic plates for radiation of varying frequencies had been determined

having frequencies within the interval under consideration (Dorgelo, 1924;
Ornstein, 1925).
