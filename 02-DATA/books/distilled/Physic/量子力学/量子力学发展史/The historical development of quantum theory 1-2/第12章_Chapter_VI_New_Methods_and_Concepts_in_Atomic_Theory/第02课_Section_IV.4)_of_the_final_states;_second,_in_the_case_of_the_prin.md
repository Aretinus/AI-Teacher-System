As we have seen, the dispersion of light by atoms constituted one example of a
general class of quantum phenomena determined by the interaction of radiation
and matter. In a programmatic essay, entitled 'Über die Wirkung von Atomen bei
Stößen' ('On the Action of Atoms in Collisions') and submitted by the end of
March 1925 to Zeitschrift für Physik, Niels Bohr discussed another example: the
passage of charged particles through matter (Bohr, 1925a). Experiments on the
scattering of charged particles, like electrons and a-particles by atoms, had
revealed the structure of atoms in the first two decades of the twentieth century.
Especially, Rutherford had formulated his model of the nuclear atom on the
basis of his investigations of the collisions between a-particles and heavy atoms
(Rutherford, 191 la,b). A little later, the experiments on the impacts of electrons
on atoms—performed by James Franck and Gustav Hertz (1914b)—had
confirmed Bohr's theory of the stationary states of atoms. While the scattering of
a-particles with atomic nuclei had been described satisfactorily with the help of
the laws of classical electrodynamics, the understanding of the inelastic electron-
atom collisions required the use of quantum laws in order to account for the
transfer of discrete energy-quanta to atoms. In spring 1925 Niels Bohr proposed
a general approach to all quantum-theoretical scattering problems, based on the
following ideas. He first suggested that the collision processes between electrons
and atoms that were usually considered—i.e., those processes which implied the
excitation of the atoms—exhibited a property which he called reciprocity
('Reziprozität,' Bohr, 1925a, p. 142). By reciprocity he denoted the fact, observed
earlier by Oskar Klein and Svein Rosseland (1921), that to each inelastic collision
between atoms and electrons, in which energy was transferred to the atom, there
existed the inverse reaction, i.e., an atom-electron collision ('collision of the
second kind'), in which a discrete amount of energy was transferred from the
atom to the electron, leaving the atom in a final state of lower energy without
emission of radiation ('radiationless collision'). The reciprocity property was not,
in Bohr's opinion, shared by the processes between radiation and atoms, such as
the dispersion of light, which were described by Kramers' dispersion formula, Eq.
(348).

The year 1923, in which the failures of atomic models to account for the energy

represented a low point in the development of atomic theory. These serious
difficulties did not, however, stop the experimental and theoretical research in
spectroscopy. On the contrary, a boom of new investigations followed in the next
few years, for the physicists were determined to crack the puzzles of atomic
structure by exploring the finest details of line and band spectra with improved
apparatus and intricate model considerations. Numerous centres devoted to

Working with the above hypothesis, Stoner proposed a new classification
exhibiting two features: first, 'the number of electrons in each completed level is
equal to double the sum of the inner quantum numbers as assigned, there being
in the K, L, M, N levels, when complete, 2,8(2 + 2 + 4), 18(2 + 2 + 4 + 4 + 6),

p. 722). This classification immediately explained the ground-state structure of
the atoms of noble gases: in helium the AMevel with inner quantum number j = 1
is occupied by two electrons; in neon, in addition to the completed AT-shell (with
two electrons), the L-shell is occupied with two (for the L(K = \, j = 1) state)
plus two (for the L(2,1) state) plus four (for the L(2,2) state), i.e., with eight
electrons; in argon one has also the Af-level occupied with ten (four for the
Af(3,2) state and six for the A/(3,3) state), hence there are altogether eighteen
electrons, etc. Stoner's classification, of course, deviated from Bohr's. In Bohr's
theory of the periodic system the form of the orbit had played the crucial role:
thus, for instance, in the L-levels, one distinguished only between the nk = 2r
and 22-orbits, and assumed that both types had to be occupied (in a complete
shell) by four electrons each. For obtaining these occupation numbers of the
j^-levels, Bohr had used detailed energy considerations in the case of competing
orbits, and from such considerations it followed occasionally that the maximum
occupation number of a given %-state was different for atoms with different
atomic numbers: especially, the inner sublevels of elements having higher atomic
numbers could be filled by more electrons than were present in a complete level
of an element having a lower atomic number. In contrast to that, Stoner's scheme
was characterized by the fact that 'inner sub-groups [i.e., states with given
principal and inner quantum numbers, n andy] are completed at an earlier stage,
subsequent changes being made by simple addition of electrons to outer sub-
levels without reorganization of the groups as a whole' (Stoner, 1924, p. 724).
The crucial role in the new organization of the inner atomic structure was
assumed by the inner quantum number, which Sommerfeld had postulated and
which had been used to classify line multiplets and the anomalous Zeeman

components of many-electron atoms. The similarity of the optical and the X-ray
spectra justified to some extent the application of the inner quantum number in
the description of the inner structure of atoms. Although so far no unique
physical interpretation had been given of the inner quantum number—
Sommerfeld's original interpretation of it as the spatial quantum number
determining the spatial orientation did not identify it clearly enough for atoms with
many electrons—Stoner's scheme seemed to establish the inner quantum number
further as an independent and fundamental quantum number in atomic
structure. From the fact that 2/ electrons could be in a complete subgroup denoted by
the index j, Stoner concluded that in the subgroup up to 2/ equally probable
states existed.
In the last section of his paper, Section 7, Stoner reported the evidence
supporting the proposed electron distribution in atoms. First of all, he quoted the
information derived from the relative intensity of X-ray lines. Assuming this
intensity was—more or less—proportional to the number of electrons in the
levels involved in the transition, he derived from the fact, that the Ka -line of
several metals (such as iron, copper, etc.) was about half as intense as the
Ka -component, the consequence that in the L(2,2)-state (which was assumed to
be involved in the Ka -transition) twice as many electrons existed as in the
L(2, l)-state (which was involved in the Ka -transition). That is, the intensity ratio
proved the new electron distribution to be the correct one rather than Bohr's
(according to which a different intensity ratio, either 3:1 or 1:1 would be
expected). He found a similarly positive result from the other available intensity
data.1004 Second, Stoner quoted the data on the ionic paramagnetism of the
third-period elements, from K + , Ca+ +, . . . to Cu+, as confirming his views on
the systematic occupation of the A/(3,2)- and M(3,3)-levels in the atoms of
successive elements in the periodic system of elements. As we have mentioned
earlier, the building-up of the electron groups in Bohr's theory was connected
with a complete reorganization of the whole M-group, which made the observed
regularity in the number of Bohr magnetons—according to observation this
number increased in steps of unity from 0 to 5 (for Mn+ + and Fe+ + + with 23

The progress in atomic theory described above rested on the use of relationships
involving integral numbers, such as the number of electrons occupying a given
energy level in the atom under investigation. It happened at times that the
quantum laws of this kind did not reflect the dynamical situation in atoms
properly, especially the fact that the electrons moved on orbits under the
attractive force of nuclei and the repulsion of neighbouring electrons. One could
hardly expect that such a dynamical situation would yield relationships describ-
able in terms of ratios of small integral numbers, like the ones accounting for the
intensity ratios of multiplet components. On the contrary, one could foresee that
the electron-electron interaction in many electron atoms must destroy any
simple ratios. If one further believed in the principles of the Bohr-Sommerfeld
atomic theory, one had real difficulty in answering the question of why the
dynamical perturbation due to the mutual interaction of electrons should not
play the expected role. The problem was even more complicated. Bohr himself
had claimed repeatedly in the early 1920s that, in order to understand atomic
structure and atomic spectra in detail, one had to take into account the
interaction between the charged particles constituting atoms and their couplings
to the radiation field. And the progress achieved with the help of the dispersion-
theoretic approach in explaining various properties of spectral lines justified his
claim. Now the similarly successful Main Smith-Stoner-Pauli theory of atomic
structure did not refer to any interaction or coupling in atoms. Pauli stated
clearly that the new approach had nothing to do with the previous dynamical
picture of atoms, which he believed to be fundamentally wrong. The physicists
around Bohr in Copenhagen, while acknowledging the progress brought about by
the ideas of Stoner and Pauli, judged the old atomic theory to be better than
what Pauli thought. They rather agreed with what Pauli had himself cautiously
stated in his paper of December 1924, after having discussed the respective

Some of the same questions, e.g., the explanation of the relativistic doublets of
X-ray spectra and of the anomalous Zeeman effects on the basis of the
hypothesis of the magnetic electron, were treated by F. Russell Bichowski and Harold
Urey in a paper, which they communicated to the Proceedings of the National
Academy of Sciences, where it appeared in the issue of February 1926 (Bichowski
and Urey, 1926).1069'1070 Bichowski and Urey referred to Uhlenbeck and
Goudsmit's note in Naturwissenschaften and applied the conception of the
magnetic electron to calculate explicitly the double separation due to different
relative orientations of the electron's magnetic moment and the orbit. Thus, by
assuming the magnetic moment to be (e/2mec) • (h/2ir)—i.e., half as big as the
value assumed by Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit—they obtained the same expression
as on the relativistic theory. While this result fitted the alkali doublets and the
X-ray doublets, it seemed to Bichowski and Urey to be incompatible with the
fine structure of the hydrogen lines: because in that case, they claimed, one had
to take a superposition of Sommerfeld's relativistic separation (the relativistic
doublet) plus the separation of Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit, arising from the
interaction of the magnetic moment of the electron with the internal field.1071
Like Bichowski and Urey, also Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit had trouble
describing the fine structure of the hydrogen lines. The reasons, however, were different.
