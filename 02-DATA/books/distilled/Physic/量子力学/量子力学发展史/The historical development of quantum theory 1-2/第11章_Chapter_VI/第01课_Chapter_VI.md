# Chapter VI

New Methods and Concepts in Atomic Theory
In Chapters IV and V we have discussed the difficulties of the standard
Bohr-Sommerfeld theory of atomic structure, which had arisen since 1922 in the
detailed investigation of several important problems. In this chapter we shall deal
with certain specific developments, which occurred mainly in 1924 and 1925 and
seemed to open the path to a more satisfactory description of spectroscopic data,
namely: the dispersion-theoretic approach to atomic theory; the new
organization of electron groups in atoms and, associated with it, a new principle, later
called the exclusion principle; and, finally, the concept of electron spin.
The dispersion-theoretic approach developed from the endeavour to obtain
the correct theory of the dispersion of light by matter, whose atoms were
described by Bohr's theory of atomic structure. After several attempts since 1913
had failed, Rudolf Ladenburg was the first to derive in 1921 a quantum-
theoretical formula. This formula was extended and completed in early 1924 by
Hendrik Kramers. And Kramers' dispersion formula became the prototype of
many further quantum formulae, which allowed one to describe properties
arising from the interaction of atoms with radiation, such as the width of spectral
lines or the energy loss of charged particles passing through matter. The
quantum-theoretical dispersion formula thus developed into a general scheme, a
scheme formulated with the help of a peculiar mathematical method: according
to it the dynamical equations of the old atomic theory, which were differential
equations, were transformed into difference equations. Since the dispersion
approach played the principal role in the discovery of quantum mechanics,
which we shall discuss in Volume 2, we restrict ourselves in what follows to
outlining the prehistory of Kramers' dispersion formula and to sketching in brief
the problems of atomic structure to which this approach could be successfully
applied.
While Bohr and his associates put their hopes in an extended use of the
correspondence principle, other prominent quantum physicists—including
Arnold Sommerfeld and Wolfgang Pauli—had serious doubts about the further
usefulness of this principle in atomic theory. They argued, in particular, that the
correspondence principle could hardly account for the numerical relationships
exhibited by the data. In the description of data, ratios of small integral numbers
occurred frequently; these ratios seemed to be connected with the peculiar
organization of electrons in stable orbits, which was not explained by the existing
atomic theory nor by any correspondence argument. From the analysis of
chemical and spectroscopic data, J. D. Main Smith and Edmund Stoner derived

In connection with the exclusion principle, Pauli introduced a new quantum

momentum, or spin, did not solve all the difficulties of the theory of multiplets
and of the anomalous Zeeman effects, it enabled the spectroscopists to order
satisfactorily most of the available, relevant spectroscopic data. Hence the
concept of spin, together with the exclusion principle, both of which had not
been anticipated on the basis of any principle of the Bohr-Sommerfeld theory of
atomic structure, represented an important extension of it. If to the theory so
extended one added the dispersion-theoretic approach, a modified Bohr-
Sommerfeld theory seemed to arise, which represented another step towards the
future quantum theory of atomic structure.
VI. 1 The Dispersion-Theoretic Approach
The dispersion of light when passing through matter without getting fully
absorbed, i.e., the variation of the refractive index n (which denotes the ratio of
the velocity of light in vacuo to the velocity of light in matter) with the
wavelength, always played an important role in the history of optics since the
times of Isaac Newton. After the undulatory theory of light won over the
corpuscular view in the beginning of the nineteenth century, Augustin Louis
Cauchy proposed an explanation of the dispersion phenomenon, based on the
assumption that matter consisted of molecules having finite distances from each
other (Cauchy, 1836). According to his theory the refractive index n increased
with decreasing wavelength X of the light—n was approximately proportional to
X-2—in agreement with the data on what was later called 'normal' dispersion.
When studying the spectrum obtained from the transmission of light through a
prism filled with iodine vapour, F.-P. Le Roux discovered an inversion of the
usual order of colours, i.e., blue light was deflected more strongly than red; he
called this phenomenon 'anomalous dispersion' (Le Roux, 1862). A closer
investigation of anomalous dispersion yielded the following result: the phenomenon
was always observed with radiation having wavelength around an absorption line
of the dispersive medium; then the refractive index reached very high values for
