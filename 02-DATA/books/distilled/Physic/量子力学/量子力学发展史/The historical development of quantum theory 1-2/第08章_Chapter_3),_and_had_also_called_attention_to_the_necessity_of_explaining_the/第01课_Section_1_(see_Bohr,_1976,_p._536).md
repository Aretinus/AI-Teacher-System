Klein had thought about the crossed-field problem already for some time. The
reason was that before going to the United States he had met Erik Hulthen in
Lund, who got him interested in the theory of molecules. This led him to search
for a simple analogy of the rotating molecule. As he recalled later: 'I thought that
if you take the hydrogen atom, and then if you have an electric field, that would
be a little like the action of the other atom on the electron of the first atom; and
the magnetic field would be a little like the rotation [of the molecule]' (Klein,
Conversations, p. 75). Klein was aware of Bohr's claim that the crossed-field

fundamental principles of atomic theory, one should always be able to obtain
from a given quantum state another state by an adiabatic transformation, which
implied that both states should exist theoretically. However, one had reasons to
exclude from consideration certain orbits as being inadmissible: e.g., the
pendulum orbits of the electron, which could approach the atomic nucleus closely. This
restriction also excluded the orbits with m = 0, i.e., the pendulum orbits parallel
to the direction of the magnetic field. And if these had to be excluded, then the
adiabatic hypothesis also broke down in the simple case of the hydrogen atom
under the simultaneous influence of electric and magnetic fields.
Pauli concluded his treatment of the crossed-field problem with the remark:
'An escape from this difficulty can be achieved only by a radical change in the
foundation of the theory' (Pauli, 1926b, p. 164). However, at that time—i.e., in
summer 1925—not only had the failure of the mechanical principles of atomic
theory been demonstrated in great detail, but also the special assumptions made
about radiation had been proved to be questionable. This situation was brought
about by the new experimental and theoretical results bearing on the nature of
radiation and its interaction with matter, which we shall discuss next.
