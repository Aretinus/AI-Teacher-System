Thus, the theory developed by his teacher David Hilbert twenty years earlier
provided the clue to solving the most important problem of quantum mechanics:
the calculation of the energy states of atomic systems. Moreover, it seemed to
remove another fundamental difficulty of the old atomic theory that lay in
different treatments of discrete and continuous states.
The endeavours of Born, Heisenberg and Jordan led to the development of
the theory of matrix mechanics, which was applicable to all types of multiply
periodic systems, to nondegenerate and degenerate ones, and in principle even to
aperiodic systems. In addition, the authors realized that the matrix equations had
a simpler structure than the corresponding classical equations. Thus, for example,
the set of Hamilton-Jacobi partial differential equations of classical mechanics
was replaced by a set of algebraic equations; their solution established a unitary
transformation of the quantum-mechanical Hamiltonian matrix to a diagonal
matrix. Now the discussion of conservation laws also appeared to be
considerably more elementary. The three co-authors succeeded in presenting the theory in
a finished and compact form. Four years later Born and Jordan took it over
unchanged into their book Elementare Quantenmechanik (1930). This book
reflected on every page the debt to the mathematical foundation of quantum
mechanics that Hilbert had provided in his theory of integral equations. But it
also emphasized the other, the physical foundation of the new atomic theory,
which had come from Niels Bohr's correspondence approach, for as Born and
Jordan remarked in their preface:
Indeed, we see in the new mechanics the rigorous realization of Bohr's programme.
We believe that the new mechanics does not represent an approximation to classical
ideas, nor any departure from Bohr's conceptions (stationary states, quantum
jumps, transition probabilities, etc.), but rather their [i.e., of Bohr's conceptions]
systematic extension to a logically consistent system. We have expressed this by
asking the founder of modern atomic theory [i.e., Bohr] to allow us to dedicate this
book to him. (Born and Jordan, 1930, p. VI)
On receiving a copy of the book, Heisenberg wrote to his former collaborators: 'I
have been especially happy about the dedication to Bohr. One must at least once
publicly emphasize that all the essential ideas of Bohr have also been taken over
into the new quantum mechanics' (Heisenberg to Born and Jordan, 20 January
1930).

the formulation of matrix mechanics (Born and Jordan, 1925b). At the same time
he learned that not only did Heisenberg agree with their work, but had also
started to concern himself with the matrix formalism. He was happy to see that
Heisenberg had joined his and Jordan's efforts and that an intensive
collaboration on the new theory was developing. He regretted, of course, that Heisenberg
could participate in the work only by mail from Copenhagen. After some time he
felt that all of them needed closer contact, and he wrote to Niels Bohr: 'Now I
must ask you not to take it amiss that I have suggested to Heisenberg to come to

Born had good reason to request Heisenberg's presence in Göttingen at that
moment.105 It was necessary to coordinate as much as possible the ideas and
intentions of the three collaborators. Heisenberg and Jordan had never worked
together before; their interests had been different and, when Jordan completed
his doctorate under Born in fall 1924, Heisenberg had been away in Copenhagen
for a long stay. But even Born and Heisenberg had not collaborated actively for
more than a year; the two of them had treated different problems of atomic
theory and followed ideas which seemed to go in different directions. Now in fall
1925 the situation had changed: Born discovered that Heisenberg's and his own
ideas in quantum mechanics could be formulated systematically in terms of
matrices, and Jordan had proved to be his most able helper in doing so. The
success of Born and Jordan had convinced Heisenberg into joining the
bandwagon of matrix mechanics. However, in spite of their common interest, the
three of them had somewhat different goals in mind. Jordan frequently
supported Born's intention of establishing a quantum-mechanical scheme to replace
classical mechanics (augmented by quantum conditions) that would be as
complete and general as possible. Born and Jordan thus wished to fulfill the aim of
their first paper (Born and Jordan, 1925b), which was to erect the matrix
formulation of quantum mechanics on the basis of a minimum set of
assumptions and to extend the formalism to include also (nonrelativistic and relativistic)
systems of several, even infinite, degrees of freedom. On the other hand,
Heisenberg, once his call for a consistent mathematical scheme had been
answered, was less interested in the mathematical foundations of matrix
mechanics and much more in its applications to actual problems. In doing so he felt that
one should not leave for the moment the familiar domain of previously observed
and treated quantum phenomena, like the ones connected with the emission and
absorption of spectral lines and the dispersion of light by atoms. Apart from
verifying old results, such as the Kramers-Heisenberg dispersion formulae, by
means of the new formalism, he wanted to move closer to the ambition he had
entertained earlier in May of that year: that of working out a theory of the
hydrogen spectrum. Thus he remarked to Pauli: To be sure, the most important
thing is still missing, namely the actual integration [of the equations of matrix
mechanics] in the case of hydrogen' (Heisenberg to Pauli, 18 September 1925). It
was for this purpose that he was eager to establish the quantum conditions (121)
for systems of several degrees of freedom. Hence he reproached Jordan, who
thought of attacking also the problems of electrodynamics—which implied
systems of infinitely many degrees of freedom—by writing: 'As for the matrix

that needed straightening out. These concerned both matters of principle and
procedure. Born and Jordan had constructed the new theory by setting up a
formal mathematical scheme from which the physical results, such as the
conservation of energy, could be deduced. In doing so they had emphasized, in
particular, the fundamental difference between their approach and the earlier
one based on the correspondence principle. Heisenberg, who had followed the
Copenhagen tradition of employing the correspondence principle in his own

more specific point, on which Heisenberg disagreed with Born and Jordan, was
