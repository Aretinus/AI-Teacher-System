II. 1 The Programme of Matrix Mechanics
At the outset of their paper, 'Zur Quantenmechanik' ('On Quantum Mechanics'),
which was received by Zeitschrift für Physik on 27 September 1925, Born and
Jordan stated their programme as follows:
The theoretical approach of Heisenberg [1925c] recently published in this Journal,
which aimed at setting up a new kinematical and mechanical formalism in
conformity with the basic requirements of quantum theory, appears to us of
considerable potential significance. It represents an attempt to render justice to the new
facts by setting up a new and really suitable conceptual system instead of adapting
the customary conceptions in a more or less artificial and forced manner. The
physical reasoning which led Heisenberg to his development has been so clearly
described by him that any supplementary remarks appear superfluous. But, as he
himself indicates, in its formal, mathematical aspects his approach is but in its
initial stages. His hypotheses have been applied only to simple examples without
being fully carried through to a generalized theory. Having been in an
advantageous position to familiarize ourselves with his ideas throughout their formative
stages, we now strive (since his investigations have been concluded) to clarify the
mathematically formal content of his approach and present some of our results
here. These indicate that it is in fact possible, starting with the basic premises given
by Heisenberg, to build up a closed mathematical theory of quantum mechanics
which displays strikingly close analogies with classical mechanics, but at the same
time preserves the characteristic features of quantum phenomena. (Born and
Jordan, 1925b, p. 858)
At the start of their investigation Born and Jordan found themselves in a
somewhat difficult situation because Heisenberg, who had initiated the physical
ideas, was away from Göttingen and therefore unavailable for discussions. They
could not even ask him whether he agreed with their continuation of his work or
whether he would prefer to proceed alone in a different direction. Born was
conscious of this situation, especially since Pauli had warned him to keep his
hands off and not spoil Heisenberg's physical ideas by mathematical formalism.
On the other hand, he could, with clear conscience, point to the fact that
Heisenberg had given him a finished paper in which he had written down all his
results and that this paper would be published before long; hence every physicist
Heisenberg mentioned disapprovingly to Pauli (Heisenberg to Pauli, 16 November 1925). In his
review of Bom's Problems of Atomic Dynamics (Born, 1926d), Edwin C. Kemble then spoke explicitly
about 'matrix mechanics' (Kemble, 1926b, p. 424). After 1927 the notion of 'matrix mechanics'
became standard in the literature (see, e.g., Allen, 1927, p. 78, or Thirring, 1928, p. 405). Later, even
Born and Jordan used it in their book Elementare Quantenmechanik (1930).

In August 1925 Jordan had already developed the matrix scheme so far that
he could proceed to give a general proof of energy conservation and of the
frequency condition in quantum mechanics. Naturally Heisenberg, who, in his
example of the anharmonic oscillator had only given the proof in second-order
perturbation theory, was extremely interested in these results. He learned that
Born and Jordan had conceived a much more ambitious scheme of quantum
mechanics than he had considered in his previous paper (Heisenberg, 1925c).
There he had employed the Newtonian equation of motion to describe specific
examples of mechanical systems, such as the anharmonic oscillator and the
rotator. He had not referred, however, to the Hamiltonian scheme, which had
provided the basis of the old atomic theory. To Born it was evident that one had
to employ the Hamiltonian scheme in the new theory also. 'I had written this
book on atomic mechanics [Born, 1925] completely from the Hamiltonian
standpoint,' he said later, 'and I was convinced that the best way to present all
the fundamental laws [of quantum mechanics] was the Hamiltonian form' (Born,
Conversations, p. 39). Therefore the task which he set himself and Jordan was to
formulate a new 'quantum mechanics' by re-interpreting the classical dynamical
laws of Newton and Hamilton in terms of matrix equations.
Born and Jordan did not wish to confine themselves merely to translating
Heisenberg's considerations in the language of matrices, in order 'to clarify the
formal content of his approach,' although that task would have served as a valid
motivation for their undertaking. Rather, their intention was to obtain the

expressed as functions of qk, the generalized position coordinates, and pk, their
conjugate momenta, with k = 1, . . . , n. Hence Born and Jordan, in their re-

mathematical tools for doing so were available in matrix algebra, which they adapted
for their purposes. The reformulation of the equations of mechanics and
electrodynamics in the language of matrices required two other operations which did
not seem to be available and had to be defined: these were the differentiation of
a dynamical variable (expressed as a matrix) with respect to time, and the
differentiation of a matrix function with respect to one of its (matrix) variables.
By introducing both of these differential operations Born and Jordan completed
the formal tools necessary for the reformulation of the dynamical laws in matrix
form.

The discussion of (matrix) functions of matrices signified much more than
merely a technical point in the work of Born and Jordan. The development of
classical mechanics from Newton to Hamilton had yielded the fundamental
result that every dynamical variable of a given dynamical system could be
expressed as a function of the sets of generalized position and momentum
coordinates, qk and pk (k = 1, . . . , n\ where n is the number of degrees of
freedom. These functions representing the dynamical variables have the general
form of series of products of powers of the qk and pk. In formulating the new
quantum mechanics rationally, Born had decided to stay as close to Hamiltonian
mechanics as possible. Hence the recipe he and Jordan followed was to
reinterpret all the classical dependences of dynamical variables, say yh on the
canonical variables, say xk (i.e., the set of all qk and pk of the system under
consideration), whose matrix form could be expressed by Eqs. (72). The
important result of their investigation was that the reformulation could be achieved by
taking into account the fundamental property of matrices, i.e., their noncom-
mutativity, which had been known since the work of Cayley. The noncommuta-
tivity had been first discovered by Heisenberg to hold for dynamical variables in
atomic systems. Born and Jordan went beyond Heisenberg by asking the
principal question: Given any classical dynamical variable, is it possible to
obtain the corresponding matrix expression by means of well-defined operations?
In order to answer this question they had to go beyond the known rules of matrix
algebra because the classical laws relating physical quantities or variables also
involve other mathematical processes, such as the differentiation with respect to
a parameter or a variable. What corresponded to such operations in the matrix
theory?

II.3 Dynamical Laws and Energy Conservation
In order to formulate quantum mechanics in matrix form, Born and Jordan first
assembled the known facts of matrix algebra and added a few new definitions,
such as the one concerning matrix differentiation.82 With the help of these purely
mathematical concepts and methods they then proceeded to develop matrix
mechanics. As a first step, they looked for the proper expressions for
Heisenberg^ ideas in matrix language; after that they generalized these results. They
discovered immediately that, instead of using the Newtonian equations of motion
as Heisenberg had done, they could equally well start from the Hamiltonian
scheme of dynamics. They showed, in particular, that the quantum dynamical
laws followed, like the corresponding classical ones, from a fundamental
variational principle. Moreover, they found that one of the basic conservation laws of
classical mechanics, the one ensuring energy conservation, also held in quantum
mechanics, and that the quantum-theoretical frequency condition followed
automatically from their matrix scheme.83

that type—i.e., Hermitian forms whose real and imaginary parts are separately
compact—possess a discrete spectrum of eigenvalues and can be reduced to a
sum of squares. Now in quantum mechanics Hermitian forms, or the related
matrices or operators, would find their most important application and become
known to all theoretical physicists.

II.3 Dynamical Laws and Energy Conservation
Quantum mechanics was, of course, not determined completely by
reformulating the equations of motion of the corresponding classical theory in matrix form.
In addition, the dynamical matrix variables, q and p, of a quantum system (with
one degree of freedom) had to satisfy the re-interpreted quantum condition, Eq.
(67). This matrix equation, which they called the 'sharpened' (verschärfte,9 Born
and Jordan, 1925b, p. 871) quantum condition, yielded important information
about the structure of matrices which represent the variables in quantum
mechanics. By taking the trace on both sides of Eq. (67), Born and Jordan showed
that the matrices p and q could not be finite because for two finite matrices the
difference of the traces D(p,q) and D(q, p) must be zero. Even more, the matrix
products did not even have finite traces and the same held for their difference.
Hence Born and Jordan concluded that 'they do not belong to the class of
"bounded" infinite matrices hitherto almost exclusively investigated by
mathematicians' (Born and Jordan, 1925b, p. 870, footnote (1)). This observation
confronted the physicists with serious mathematical difficulties because they
were not allowed to take over the well-known mathematical results of the theory
of infinite matrices—such as Hilbert's (1906a)—but had to examine carefully the
theorems used in each case separately. Their situation resembled somewhat the
one which G. W. Hill had encountered fifty years earlier in dealing with infinite
determinants in lunar theory; and, like Hill, Born and Jordan had now to justify
their mathematical procedure by pointing to the reasonableness of the physical
results obtained therewith. In the early steps of the matrix formulation of
quantum-mechanical relations, they could only hope that their methods would be
justified a posteriori. Their lack of caution in running around the minefield of the
theory of infinite matrices, picking and choosing what might be useful, only
enhanced their confidence as pioneers in the new theory of quantum
mechanics.88

II.5 Preliminary Remarks on Radiation
Born and Jordan's first goal had been to give a mathematically secure
foundation to the concepts of quantum theory. Among the fundamental assumptions in
his paper on the reformulation of kinematic and mechanical relations,
Heisenberg had proposed to interpret \q(nm)\2, the square of the transition amplitudes
associated with the position coordinate of a periodic system, as the probability
for a quantum jump in the system (Heisenberg, 1925c, Section 1). In quantum
mechanics this assumption meant, of course, a bold step which required
justification. In classical theory the emission of radiation from an accelerated electron
could be calculated according to the laws of electrodynamics. Especially, there
existed a well-defined relation between the quantities characterizing the motion
of the electron and the electromagnetic fields characterizing the intensity of the
emitted radiation. In the new quantum-mechanical formalism classical
quantities, such as the acceleration of an electron, were simply represented by the
corresponding matrix quantities. Was one permitted to perform the same
reformulation with the variables of the electromagnetic field? Born and Jordan
answered this question in the affirmative, but they immediately added that 'the
considerations advanced here have only a provisional character' (Born and
Jordan, 1925b, p. 883).
One of the main reasons for their cautious attitude was the fact that, in early
September 1925, they did not know how to formulate the quantum condition for
systems having several degrees of freedom. For the moment they bypassed this
difficulty by restricting themselves to free cavity radiation, which may be treated
as a system of uncoupled oscillators, each possessing a fixed frequency. As they
argued:
There is hardly any doubt as to how to treat this system. For this purpose the fact,
that the fundamental equations of electrodynamics are linear (superposition
principle), is of special significance; because from that it follows that the substitute
("Ersatz") oscillators [representing the cavity radiation] are harmonic, and just in
the case of harmonic oscillators—in contrast to other systems—energy conservation
is valid independently of the quantum condition . . . One would therefore expect
that one may similarly obtain the integral theorems of the electrodynamics of
vacuum (energy and momentum conservation) quite generally by solely
reformulating Maxwell's equations in matrix language, without taking into account the

quantum condition. By demonstrating this we simultaneously gain the means of
justifying Heisenberg's assertion about the significance of \q(nm)\2. (Born and
Jordan, 1925b, p. 884)96
