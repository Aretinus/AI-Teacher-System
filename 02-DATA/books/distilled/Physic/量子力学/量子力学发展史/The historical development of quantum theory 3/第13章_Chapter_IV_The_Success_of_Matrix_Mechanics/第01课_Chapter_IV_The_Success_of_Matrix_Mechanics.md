Born, Heisenberg and Jordan formulated the problem of the action of
time-dependent forces on atoms in quantum mechanics ' ... as the limiting case
of interaction between two systems in which the influence of the interaction on
one of the two systems (called system A) is so small that the action upon the
other (system B) remains unaffected by this influence' (BHJ, 1926, p. 568). In
classical mechanics the total Hamiltonian function describing the two systems is
then given by a sum of three terms, say, H = HA+ XHB + XeHAB, where the first,
HA, denotes the energy of free system A, the second, XHB, the energy of the free
system B, and the third, XeHAB, the coupling or interaction energy. The third
term is supposed to be a correction, which is small compared to the total energy
and to the energies HA and XHB (i.e., € < 1). Now the system A is taken to be
known. Then the motion of system B may be determined by the Hamiltonian
function XHB + XeHAB, where the interaction energy term, XeHAB, depends, of
course, on the coordinates of the system A. The latter coordinates may be time
dependent; however, for a fixed time /, only given values of these coordinates
(and their conjugate momenta) enter into the classical calculation. If the reaction
of the system B on system A may be neglected—say, due to the fact that system
B has a much smaller energy than A (or A< 1)—then the motion of B is

where the interaction term depends explicitly on time / (which takes into account
the dependence on the variables of A). Thus, an adequate classical description of
the action of an external time-dependent force on system B is provided.
Born, Heisenberg and Jordan then turned to the discussion of the same
problem in quantum mechanics. They began by noting:

restrict ourselves to first-order perturbations (i.e., terms proportional to € in the
coordinates and momenta, etc., of the system B). It is altogether different, however,

products of quantities in which more than one implicitly contains the coordinates of
A. But this means, according to the quantum mechanical rule of building a product,
that it by no means suffices to know the "external forces as functions of time"

IV. 1 The Treatment of Dispersion Phenomena
As mentioned above, the difficulties with the concept of external forces did
not show up in the lowest-order approximation; in that case one neglects the
reaction of system B on A, which leads to a change of the variables of A, and this
change, in turn, induces additional terms of order Xe2 in the variables of B.170
The latter result even allows one to make a more precise statement concerning
the concept of external forces in quantum mechanics. Evidently, it makes sense
to talk about external forces if the action of the system A on the system B can be
described in a manner independent of the dynamical variables of B. This is
especially so when the parameter A, which characterizes the influence of the
relative magnitude of the system B with respect to the system A, is very small.
The authors noted, however, that 'in this approximation the quantum mechanical
construction of products, and thereby the calculation of the perturbation to
higher orders in € can again be effected. In fact, the rules for this building of
products reduce simply to those of classical multiplication, as in this
approximation the coordinates, amplitudes and frequencies which enter into Y\AB do not
depend on the constants in A' (BHJ, 1926, p. 569).
A careful examination of the conditions, under which the concept of external
forces had a well-defined meaning in their theory, enabled Born, Heisenberg and
Jordan to suggest that certain important problems of atomic physics could
perhaps be solved by applying a time-dependent quantum-mechanical
perturbation theory. In particular, they argued that:
In this sense one could, for example, treat the action of a strong alternating
electromagnetic field on an atom indeed as the influence of an "external force" by
neglecting the reaction of the atom [on the incident electromagnetic radiation], since
the field energy can be regarded as infinitely large compared with that of the atom.
The action of a-particles upon the electrons of an atom could also be viewed as an
"external force," as in classical theory, because of the large energy of the a-
particles; in this approximation, the Fourier expansion in time of the force thereby
exerted upon the electrons would also be the same as in classical theory. (BHJ,
1926, p. 569)
On the other hand, the authors were very well aware of the fact that an approach
of that kind was not applicable to some other phenomena: for instance, the
action of forces due to one atom upon another could not be treated as an
operation of external forces (except in the first-order terms) since neglecting the
reaction in higher-order terms would lead to wrong results.171

IV.2 Fluctuations in Cavity Radiation
In October 1925 Heisenberg reported to Pauli:
Another thing, which Jordan has [especially] done for our paper is [to treat] the
statistical behaviour of eigenvibrations, say of a membrane, in the new theory.
Jordan claims that one obtains for the interference fluctuations the right result, i.e.,
both the classical and the Einstein terms, and he believes that he sees an analogy of
our calculations with Bose's statistics. I am a little unhappy about it, for I do not
understand statistics enough in order to decide whether there is something
reasonable in it, but I cannot also criticize it, because I find the problem itself and the
calculation significant. (Heisenberg to Pauli, 23 October 1925)180
The extension of matrix mechanics to systems of many degrees of freedom had
provided the necessary tool for investigating the problem of cavity radiation from
a fresh point of view. What Heisenberg, at the time, considered a 'significant
problem and calculation,' Pascual Jordan, the author of this investigation, later
on described as his 'most important contribution to quantum mechanics' (Jordan
to van der Waerden, 10 April 1962).
In his quantum-mechanical treatment of radiation, Jordan developed a
consistent 'wave' theory of cavity radiation, which was completely in the spirit of
Bohr's ideas and at the same time reproduced the light-quantum effects predicted
by Einstein (1909a). In order to understand the progress made by Jordan we
must recall how the radiation problem stood in summer 1925.
The theory of radiation was characterized by two distinct approaches whose
fundamental assumptions seemed to be in serious conflict with each other. On
one hand, Einstein's quantum theory of an ideal gas (Einstein, 1924c, 1925a, b)
made use of his concept of the light-quantum and the statistics introduced by

IV.3 The Conservation of Angular Momentum
Compared to the detailed formalism of matrix mechanics that had been
developed, its applications thus far had been quite poor; they were confined to
systems which consisted of harmonic or slightly anharmonic oscillators. For
instance, the derivation of the dispersion formulae implied only the use of a
harmonic oscillator model in which the transition frequencies of the scattering
atoms had been introduced as empirically known constants. However, in a
dynamical theory of atomic systems the frequencies and the intensities of the
spectral lines should actually be obtained from the equations of motion; hence
the appropriate general methods of integrating these equations had to be
developed in matrix mechanics. The hope, of course, was that these general methods
would then allow one, as in the classical case, to perform the integration for
realistic atoms and molecules having one or more electrons. In spite of serious
endeavours, Born, Heisenberg and Jordan, during their collaboration in fall
1925, did not succeed in solving any such atomic problem. They assembled all
the preliminary results obtained in that direction in Section 4.1 of the three-man

conserved quantities in quantum-mechanical problems, the authors dealt in
detail with the properties of angular momentum and a preliminary application of
their formalism to intensity problems of complex spectra. The latter parts were
worked out entirely by Werner Heisenberg and Pascual Jordan.193

These statements gave the outline of a systematic programme for developing
the theory of atomic systems within the framework of matrix mechanics. An
immediate task in this programme was to complete the discussion of the
hydrogen atom including the intensity problem and the relativistic corrections.
The next most obvious and 'elementary' task seemed to be to apply the methods
worked out by Born, Heisenberg and Jordan, and Pauli himself, to calculate the
energy states of the helium atom. Pauli knew that for all these problems he
needed an extension of the existing matrix scheme. For example, in the case of
the helium atom there was no trick available—similar to the use of the axial
(Lenz) vector in the hydrogen atom—which would help to avoid the use of action
and angle variables. Moreover, he believed firmly that one had to bring into
quantum mechanics the features described formerly by the notion of 'non-
mechanical stress.' In November 1925, right after the calculation of the hydrogen
states, he started to work hard on this programme. However, in spite of his
efforts, in which he was soon joined by Heisenberg, progress could not be made
as rapidly as he had hoped. In order to perform such tasks as the calculation of
the relativistic hydrogen atom or even the helium atom, matrix mechanics had to
be modified and extended considerably. Before that could be done, however, it
was possible to treat only one other atomic problem: the diatomic molecule.
Just when Pauli became increasingly involved in treating the problems of
atomic mechanics by using matrix formalism, Lucie Mensing, a former student

of Wilhelm Lenz' from Hamburg, learned about the new quantum mechanics in
Göttingen and became interested in applying it to calculate certain properties of

Although Pauli's earlier result had not yet been disproved empirically, Men-
sing and Pauli were convinced that the data would be represented correctly by
the quantum-mechanical result.250 They concluded: 'Hence it becomes obvious
that here, as in some other cases, the new quantum mechanics comes—with
respect to the statistical average values—closer to the results of classical
mechanics than the erstwhile quantum theory' (Mensing and Pauli, 1926, p. 512).
Independently of Lucie Mensing and Wolfgang Pauli, the problem of treating
the diatomic molecule according to the new quantum mechanics was taken up in
1926 by several authors in Europe and the United States. They not only worked
out the energy states of the rotator in matrix mechanics (see, e.g., Tamm, 1926,
and Landau, 1926), but also arrived at the above discussed formulae for the
elements of the polarization matrix a and the quantity ^z in the limit of high
temperatures (see, e.g., Van Vleck, 1926a; Kronig, 1926d).251 Thus, the rotator
became the standard system in which the theoreticians learned to use and
appreciate the tools of matrix mechanics.
