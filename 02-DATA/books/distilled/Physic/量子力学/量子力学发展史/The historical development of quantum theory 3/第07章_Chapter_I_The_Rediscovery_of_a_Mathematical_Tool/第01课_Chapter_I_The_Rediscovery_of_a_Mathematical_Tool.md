Born had barely sent off his letter to Einstein when his collaborator Werner
Heisenberg came to see him. He had in the meanwhile 'pursued some work of his
own, keeping its idea and purpose somewhat dark and mysterious,' as Born
recalled later (Born, 1978, p. 216). During the summer semester of 1925 Born had
seen less of Heisenberg than in previous times. The two of them had not worked
on common projects; actually Born had been absorbed in his official duties and
in his collaboration with Jordan on the application of dispersion-theoretic
methods to aperiodic atomic systems, while Heisenberg had devoted his efforts to
the general questions arising from the problem of calculating the intensities of
hydrogen lines, a problem which was not in the centre of Born's interests. In May
and early June 1925, before he went to Helgoland to recover from hay fever,
Heisenberg had not discussed his work with Born. As he recalled later: 'At that
time everything was so vague. We may, of course, have talked in a general
manner about what one could possibly do, but the only time when I really had
discussions about it was when he gave me this book on the Bessel functions'
(Heisenberg, Conversations, p. 269). These discussions had taken place already
earlier in May, when Heisenberg had begun by guessing the intensities of the
Balmer lines and Born had expected that Bessel functions would play a role in
solving that problem. After returning from Helgoland, Heisenberg had
communicated exclusively with Wolfgang Pauli by letters; Pauli had thus been the only
person who was familiar with the difficulties and successes of the quantum-
mechanical scheme prior to the completion of the paper (Heisenberg, 1925c).
Heisenberg had even sent the manuscript first to Hamburg, requesting Pauli's
opinion before showing it to anybody in Göttingen. Upon receiving a favourable
response from Pauli, he had then completed the paper and gone to see Born.
Born recalled the circumstances of receiving Heisenberg's paper as follows:
'He came to me with a manuscript and asked me to read it and to decide whether
it was worth publishing. At the same time he asked me for leave of absence for
the rest of the term (which ended about [1] August [1925]), as he had an

invitation to lecture at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge. He added that
though he had tried hard, he could not make any progress beyond the simple
considerations contained in his paper, and he asked me to try myself, which I
promised to do' (Born, 1978, p. 216). In spite of the fact that he 'felt that
something real was in the paper,' Heisenberg was 'very uncertain about it'
(Heisenberg, Conversations, p. 269). Hence he left the question of whether or not
the paper should be submitted to Zeitschrift für Physik to Max Born, and went
away from Göttingen without waiting for the final decision.3

The concept of matrices and the methods of operating with these objects arose in
mathematics from three sources. Perhaps the earliest was the renewed interest in
conic sections and the subsequent classification of the latter in the language of
algebra, which was basically initiated by the work of Rene Descartes in the first
half of the seventeenth century.13 More than a century later Leonhard Euler
systematically studied second degree equations in three variables x, y, z, i.e.,
equations of the form

Arthur Cayley produced a vast mathematical output over a period of fifty-
four years: 996 papers, which filled the thirteen volumes of Collected Papers
(Cayley, 1889-1898). His early work already indicated the fields, which would
interest him throughout his life: these included the geometry of plane curves, the
geometry of «-dimensions, elliptic functions and the theory of invariants. His
investigations on algebra had a particular impact. Algebra, an ancient field of
mathematics, had been greatly revived in the eighteenth century. However, in
1833 George Peacock of Cambridge University put algebra on a new level by
introducing the concept of a 'symbolic algebra,' which follows combination laws
similar to the ones used in arithmetic (Peacock, 1834).20 Peacock's extension of
algebra to objects other than ordinary numbers simplified the path to new
concepts, such as the quaternions of Hamilton or the matrices of Cayley. Also
the theory of invariants emerged as a field of algebra, and Cayley's work on this
subject attracted scholars in England (George Boole, George Salmon, Joseph
Sylvester), Germany (Alfred Clebsch, Paul Gordan), France (Camille Jordan,
Charles Hermite) and Italy (Francesco Brioschi). It then became connected with
the ideas of Bernhard Riemann, whose work would eventually prove to be one of
the cornerstones of a fundamental physical theory: the general relativity theory.
Cayley's investigations on invariants cannot be separated from his other
contributions to algebra, especially from those to matrix theory. His first paper
on matrix theory, written in French and published in a German periodical
(Cayley, 1855), already reflected the internationalism which would characterize
the later development of this field. In this paper, Cayley introduced a shorthand
(1842-1845), an appointment which might have been renewed had he cared to take the holy orders,
but Cayley did not. (Unlike his later friend Joseph Sylvester, who was not a practicing Jew but, being
Jewish, was not even allowed to compete for the Smith's Prize, Cayley was an orthodox Church of
England Christian; yet he preferred not to become a parson to keep his job.) At the age of twenty-five
Cayley left Cambridge and, being attracted by the study of law, entered Lincoln's Inn to prepare
himself for the Bar. Three years later, in 1849, he was called to the Bar and spent the following
fourteen years in the routine of legal business. Though it meant a drudgery for him, it provided him
an ample living and some spare time to devote himself to work in mathematics. At the Bar he also
became acquainted with Sylvester who shared his interest in mathematics and classical literature.
Their mathematical discussions in these years laid the foundations of the theory of algebraic
invariants which would become fruitful in many fields of mathematics. In 1863 a new mathematical
chair, the Sadlerian professorship, was established in the University of Cambridge, and Cayley, then
forty-two, accepted it though it meant a cutback in his earnings. At the same time he married Susan
Moline; they had a happy marriage, and had two children, a son and a daughter. His life in
Cambridge was devoted to mathematical research and university administration, and he had a most
successful career. Only once did he leave his university to spend, at Sylvester's invitation, six months
during 1881-1882 at Johns Hopkins University, where he lectured on Abelian functions. Cayley
received numerous honours, including the Royal medal (1859) and the Copley medal (1882) of the

1.3 Early Applications of Matrix Methods in Physics
Matrix methods had proved to be very useful in a variety of mathematical
investigations: systems of linear equations, linear differential and integral
equations, etc. But unlike other mathematical tools invented in the nineteenth
century, such as quaternions and vectors, they did not seem to arouse the interest
of physicists—notwithstanding Peter Guthrie Tait's prophecy that 'Cayley is
forging the weapons for future generations of physicists' (Tait, quoted in Knott,

occurred no physical problem in the nineteenth century, in which one sought to
make use of the specific properties of matrices, although in retrospect one might
think of the possibility of applying them in the description of symmetry
properties of crystals. When, towards the end of the nineteenth century, physicists

The use of infinite quadratic forms—especially the compact L-forms of
Toeplitz—in the lattice theory of solids by Born and von Kärmän remained
perhaps the only significant application of infinite matrices in physics until 1925.
In the years following 1912 Born had continued to develop his theory of the solid
state, going beyond the problems he had treated first with von Kärmän (i.e., the
determination of the proper vibrations in a crystal in order to calculate, say, its
specific heat). In the course of this work he occasionally referred again to the
methods of infinite matrices. For example, he employed L-forms in an
investigation of the electrostatic potential of a crystal lattice (Born, 1921). Hence Born
was perhaps the only scientist who, ever since he had learned about matrices, was
aware of their potential use in physics. It is therefore rather surprising why Born
had never applied his favourite algebraic method to problems of quantum theory
during the early 1920s. One reason for this neglect lay in Born's scientific
temperament. Whenever he pursued a new subject, as was the case with the
theory of atomic structure after 1922, he would first stick to conventional
approaches and exploit them systematically before turning to a new method.
Now in atomic theory there had been applied for many years the methods of
Hamilton and Jacobi, which seemed to be particularly adequate to describe the
dynamics of multiply periodic systems. Only after these methods were found to
be wanting did Born turn to other techniques. However, the new mechanical
description of atomic systems, which made use of the difference calculus, still
remained very close to the previous one. It was with this scheme, which Born
already called 'quantum mechanics' in spring 1924, that he had hoped to make
progress. At any rate, in summer 1925 he had not exhausted its possible
applications by far.
However, the main reason for the absence of matrix methods in the earlier
quantum theory has to be found in two facts. First, it was not known until
Heisenberg's discovery that in the description of atoms quantities, which do not
commute, play a crucial role; second, the fact that matrices are noncommuting
mathematical objects had not appeared openly in any of Born's previous
applications. Thus, Heisenberg, when he hit upon the noncommutativity property of his
quantum-theoretical quantities, did not know that there existed sophisticated
algebraic methods, which in fact had been perfected by Hilbert and his
collaborators in Göttingen, and which could be adapted to handle his problems.
Born, in turn, had mostly used matrices with very special properties—such as the
ones occurring in Toeplitz' L-forms—for physical application in his lattice

theory. Therefore, when he first read Heisenbergs paper on the re-interpretation
of mechanical relations in quantum theory, he reminded himself of the matrices
in Rosanes' lectures in Breslau rather than of the infinite quadratic forms of
Hilbert's theory of linear integral equations. After this recognition, however, he
quickly established the connection between the two; and in doing so he brought
into play all his mathematical prowess, his thorough knowledge of infinite
matrices, and his experience of the principal-axes transformations of infinite
quadratic forms.
To an outsider the progress achieved in the formulation of matrix mechanics
by Born and his collaborators from July to October 1925 appears like a marvel.
For Born, however, all the steps which he took were an almost straightforward
application of the mathematical subtleties with which he had become familiar
during the previous decade and a half. That a theoretical physicist like Born,
having just the appropriate mathematical formalism at his command, was around
at the right place in July 1925 may not have been an entirely fortuitous accident
of history. But the fact that Born possessed this knowledge explains how
Heisenbergs intuitive quantum-mechanical scheme could be transformed into a
full-fledged matrix mechanics within a short three months, when everybody—
including Heisenberg—expected years of hard intellectual labour before a
rational atomic mechanics would arise.
In spite of his mathematical preparation in matrix theory, Born did not enter
into its application to quantum mechanics alone. He knew about the complexity
of the calculations involved in such things as the principal-axis transformation of
an infinite matrix from his own work in solid state theory, and he also knew that
the application of matrix methods to quantum theory would require much more
than just calculating the spectrum of eigenvalues. Therefore, he desperately
wanted someone with experience both in quantum theory and mathematics to
assist him in formulating matrix mechanics. Wolfgang Pauli, whom he met in
Hanover on 19 July 1925, seemed to be just the right man for this enterprise, but
Pauli refused to collaborate. Born then turned to his second choice, Pascual
Jordan, who, though less experienced than Pauli, was interested in mathematical
formalism and was willing to study in particular the theory of infinite matrices
and its application to quantum mechanics.

Jordan's quick performance pleased Born, and they agreed to develop the new
theory of matrix mechanics together. After a careful study of Heisenberg's paper
Born sent it off to Zeitschrift für Physik by the end of July 1925 and left for
vacation in Switzerland. Jordan stayed behind in Germany to work on the joint
enterprise. Fortunately, he had no difficulty in using matrix methods, because he
had—in connection with the assistance he gave to Courant in the writing of
Methoden der mathematischen Physik—once consulted ('a little bit') Maxime
Bocher's Einführung in die höhere Algebra (1911), the German translation of a
book in which (finite) matrices were discussed in some detail (Böcher, 1907).
Thus, his mathematical background was just of the right kind for him to become
Born's successful principal collaborator in the formulation of matrix mechanics.73
A year later Born described his qualities as follows: '[Jordan is] perhaps not so
productive as far as practical results are concerned, but [he is] very
philosophically minded and mainly interested in fundamental questions. Besides
Heisenberg and Pauli, I consider him to be the most gifted of the younger colleagues'
(Born to Bohr, 18 December 1926).
