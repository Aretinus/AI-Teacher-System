# Chapter IV

The Success of Matrix Mechanics
After the theoretical scheme of quantum mechanics, including the matrix and the
equivalent algebraic formulations, had been established by Born, Heisenberg and
Jordan, it became necessary to deal immediately with two tasks: first, with tests
to confirm the consistency of the new theory, especially by comparing its results
with those which were considered reliable—such as the results of dispersion
theory and radiation theory; second, to extend the application of the new theory
beyond the simple examples which it had been possible to treat thus far. In both
tasks the Göttingen theoreticians received the help of other physicists who had
had close relations with them and had become familiar with their matrix methods
and early results prior to publication. It had thus been possible for these
physicists to work on quantum mechanics already, say, from October 1925,
several weeks before the three-man paper was submitted or the first paper of
Born and Jordan (1925b) appeared in print. In checking the consistency of the
new theoretical scheme, Hendrik Kramers made an interesting interpretation of
the quantum conditions or commutation relations, while Wolfgang Pauli
calculated the energy states of the hydrogen atom by employing exclusively matrix
methods.
Pauli's work on the hydrogen atom must be regarded as a real triumph of the
matrix methods, especially because he bypassed a major difficulty of the
available quantum-mechanical formalism: the obvious impossibility of describing the
action and angle variables. Ever since the days of Hamilton and Jacobi, these
variables had provided the key to the solution of multiply periodic systems; and
to avoid their use seemed to imply that one could not transfer the methods of
classical dynamics, including the perturbation scheme, to calculate the energy
states of real atoms. Pauli, however, discovered a clever method to solve the
hydrogen problem without invoking angle variables. He was thus able to diago-
nalize the Hamiltonian matrix for one-electron atoms and, in addition, to
determine their behaviour in constant external electric and magnetic fields in
perfect agreement with the experimental information. 'It was stupid,' Born
confessed with some envy about Pauli's success, 'that we could not solve the
hydrogen atom; it was straightforward' (Born, Conversations, p. 50). However,
the procedure, which worked in the case of the hydrogen atom, could not be
extended to two- and more-electron atoms. These problems had to wait until an
