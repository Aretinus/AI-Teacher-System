# Chapter V

Modifications and Extensions of Matrix Mechanics
The fact that matrix methods were available for the task of formulating the ideas,
which had emerged from the penultimate sharpening of the correspondence
principle, must be considered to be fortuitous. It enabled Born and his
collaborators to write down a scheme of the new quantum theory without having to
invent new mathematical tools. However, the theory thus developed—matrix
mechanics—did not satisfy the authors completely. Especially, when they
investigated its physical applications they quickly realized that a wider, more general
scheme was necessary in order to deal with problems in which nonlibrating
variables played a crucial role. Fortunately for the further development of
quantum mechanics such modifications were soon discovered, still before the end
of the year 1925. Interestingly enough, the ideas leading to the generalization of
matrix theory were brought in by outsiders: they were proposed by Cornelius
Lanczos in Frankfurt and by Norbert Wiener at the Massachusetts Institute of
Technology. These outsiders knew certain mathematical methods which they
considered to be superior to the matrix methods for handling the problems of
atomic theory; their methods—the field-like formulation of quantum mechanics
(Lanczos) and operator mechanics (Wiener)—would have led to far-reaching
consequences if they had been properly pursued. However, these authors
themselves did not possess the necessary knowledge of atomic physics in order to
arrive at those consequences. The experts like Born, Pauli or Heisenberg, on the
other hand, made use only of a small part of the possibilities offered by the
different mathematical schemes. Born was primarily interested in treating the
problem of uniform motion in a straight line, while Heisenberg and Pauli's minds
were totally occupied by the efforts to introduce action and angle variables into
matrix mechanics for the purpose of calculating such specific problems as the
relativistic fine structure and the anomalous Zeeman effects.
In proceeding with their programmes, Heisenberg and Pauli discovered
another important point. The existing quantum mechanics, even if generalized to
allow for the representation of nonlibration coordinates, did not suffice to
describe the atomic situation. The scheme had to be further extended to include
a new concept—the spin of the electron—which had again been proposed by two
outsiders: George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit in Ley den. But the
incorporation of electron spin into the theory did not occur smoothly: in the beginning it
seemed to create more difficulties than it removed. However, after a few months,
