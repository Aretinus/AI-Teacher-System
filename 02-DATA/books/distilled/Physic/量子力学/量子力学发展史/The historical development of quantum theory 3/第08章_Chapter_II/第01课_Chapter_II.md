# Chapter II

Matching the Tools and the Task
Werner Heisenberg had concluded his paper on the quantum-theoretical re-
interpretation of kinematic and mechanical relations by recommending 'a more
intensive mathematical investigation of the method which has been very
superficially employed here' (Heisenberg, 1925c, p. 893). He had given the completed
manuscript to Max Born, expecting that he would look into it cursorily and, if he
did not find some serious mistake, send it off for publication. Although Born had
not followed Heisenberg's work during the previous several weeks, he soon
became absorbed in his results, especially after recognizing the presence of a
well-defined mathematical scheme behind them. Towards the end of July 1925
the following situation developed in Göttingen: While Heisenberg was visiting
Leyden and Cambridge, his suggestion for the investigation of the mathematical
method underlying the re-interpretation of kinematic and mechanical relations
was taken up by Born. Born, in turn, recruited Jordan to help him in this
investigation, especially in the application of the algebra of matrices to formulate
a consistent atomic mechanics.
The prospect of using the advanced methods of matrix algebra made Born
happy. Not only did he enjoy mathematical formalism for its own sake, as he
had shown often enough in his scientific career, he had actually expected for
some years that the solution of problems in atomic physics would require the use
of a new mathematical tool. Now he realized that the new mathematical tool was
the matrix calculus, and he even happened to be acquainted with it. Born's
familiarity with the use of matrices considerably facilitated his task of
formulating Heisenberg's ideas in a consistent mathematical language. Indeed, it soon
became clear that the quantum-mechanical theory, for which physicists had
searched for many years, would be achieved faster than expected. To that end, as
a first step, one had to express Heisenberg's quantum-theoretical concepts and
relations in term of matrices; then one had to attempt to cast all other dynamical
relations into matrix form. Thus, in July 1925 Born proposed the programme of
matrix mechanics, i.e., of a reformulation of the complete scheme of classical
mechanics (including the dynamics of the electron) as a matrix scheme.74
