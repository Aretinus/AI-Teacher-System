These equations stated the noncommutativity of the Cartesian coordinates of
angular momentum in quantum mechanics, and they led to an immediate
consequence: either all three components Mx, M^ and Mz are conserved, or none
of the three components is conserved, or just one component is conserved.200 The
same theorem holds for classical systems; however, while it is proved easily in
matrix mechanics by a few algebraic steps, it takes a long computation to
establish it in classical mechanics. The reason is that the dynamical equations,
from which the conservation of a given classical variable has to be derived, are
very complicated partial differential equations; in quantum theory, on the other
hand, the corresponding dynamical equations are purely algebraic equations.
The theorem on the conservation of the components of angular momentum in
quantum theory, contained in Chapter 4 of the Born-Heisenberg-Jordan paper,
thus represented the first example of a general situation: namely, that the
conservation laws for dynamical systems can be established much more easily in
quantum mechanics than in classical mechanics.201
Before applying the matrix formalism of angular momentum to specific
systems, the authors studied the general question concerning the possible
eigenvalues of the matrices Mx, M^ and Mz. Since it is sufficient to consider the
problem just for one matrix, Mz, they proceeded in the following way. They first
treated a system whose motion is symmetrical with respect to the z-axis. As a
result of this symmetry property, Mz must be conserved at least for systems
described by Hamiltonian matrices, which are separable in the Cartesian
coordinates and momenta of their constituent particles, just as in the classical theory.202
